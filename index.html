    04 / SPEED SOLVES DISTANCE. IT DOES NOT SOLVE ARRIVAL.

    THE 0.2c
    TRAP.

    At one fifth of light speed, Proxima Centauri is only 21.2 ideal cruise years away. That number sounds like the problem has been solved. It has not. At 0.2c, the thin interstellar medium becomes a forward beam of energetic particles; a grain too small to see can arrive with the energy of heavy machinery; and a probe that cannot shed its velocity does not arrive at all. It performs a flyby and disappears.

    01 / THE VACUUM BECOMES WEATHER

    AN ATOM IS NOW A RADIATION EVENT.

    Interstellar space is sparse, not empty. At 0.2c, every stationary hydrogen or helium nucleus meets the bow of the spacecraft at roughly 19 MeV per nucleon. A single iron atom carries about 1.1 GeV of kinetic energy. One atom does not punch a macroscopic hole, but heavy ions leave concentrated ionisation tracks, displace atoms in the lattice and progressively alter electronics and shielding.

    The danger is cumulative. A published model of an Alpha Centauri crossing found gas bombardment capable of damaging roughly 0.1 mm of quartz, while dust could erode roughly 0.5 mm under its assumed interstellar columns. Those are model-dependent depths, not universal shield specifications—but they show why “almost vacuum” cannot be ignored for four light-years.

    03 / THE RARE EVENT SETS THE SHIELD

    AVERAGE DUST IS NOT THE WHOLE RISK.

    Most particles are tiny. Larger grains are much rarer—but because mass and impact energy grow with the cube of diameter, the tail of the size distribution matters disproportionately. A mission must survive not only the expected erosion but also a low-probability strike that can destroy its leading edge. That pushes the design toward a narrow frontal silhouette, sacrificial shielding, separated layers, redundant systems and—because no shield can be made arbitrarily thick—a swarm of probes rather than faith in one vehicle.

    Speed therefore creates a trade: every reduction in cruise time increases particle energy, heat, radiation damage and shielding mass. More shield then demands more launch energy. At relativistic speed, “go faster” is not a free improvement; it changes the environment the vehicle must survive.

    04 / FLYBY IS NOT ARRIVAL

    THE SECOND HALF OF THE MISSION IS BRAKING.

    A 0.2c probe crosses one astronomical unit in only 41.6 minutes. That is enough for a rapid imaging pass, not prolonged exploration. Breakthrough Starshot is consequently framed as a flyby: its laser remains at the departure system, and the craft carries no equal destination-side beam with which to stop.

    To enter a bound orbit around Proxima, the spacecraft must remove almost all of its incoming kinetic energy and match the target system's velocity. In the ideal limit, accelerating one kilogram to 0.2c requires 1.85 petajoules; braking it requires approximately the same amount again. A one-tonne vehicle therefore has a minimum acceleration-plus-braking kinetic-energy change of 3.71 × 10¹⁸ joules—about 0.89 gigatonnes of TNT equivalent—before propulsion inefficiency, reaction mass, shielding, power systems or course corrections are counted.

    Possible escape clauses all move the difficulty rather than erase it: carry propellant and pay an extreme mass ratio; build a second beam at a destination where no infrastructure exists; deploy a vast magnetic or electric sail and brake against tenuous plasma; or use stellar photons and accept a much lower injection speed. One published photon-sail study found that a sail light enough to brake into the Alpha Centauri system could enter at roughly 4.6% of light speed: about 95 years to Alpha Centauri A/B, followed by another 46 years to reach Proxima.

    THE PARADOX

    0.2c makes the distance human. It makes the vehicle nearly impossible. The honest question is not only “how fast can it go?” It is “what survives the crossing—and what makes it stop?”
